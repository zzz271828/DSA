Circulation with Demands & Applications of Network Flow

The most general form of network flow — demands, lower bounds, and powerful reductions to standard max-flow that unlock solutions to a wide class of combinatorial problems.

9.3 Recap Maximum Bipartite Matching — The Bridge to Week 11

Week 10 ended with the maximum bipartite matching problem, which served as the first demonstration of network flow's power to solve problems that on the surface appear completely unrelated to flows. This bridge motivates everything in Week 11: the real strength of network flow is that it provides a general-purpose reduction target for combinatorial feasibility and optimisation problems.

Definition — Bipartite Matching (recap)

Given a bipartite graph G = (V, E) with V = L ∪ R, a matching M is a subset of edges such that no vertex appears more than once. The maximum bipartite matching seeks the largest such M.

Reduction to Max-Flow (recap)

  1. Direct all edges from L to R.
  2. Add a super-source s connected to every vertex in L.
  3. Add a super-sink t connected from every vertex in R.
  4. Set every edge capacity to 1.
  5. Run Ford-Fulkerson. The max-flow value equals the size of the maximum matching. Saturated L-to-R edges form the matching.
Complexity

Ford-Fulkerson on the bipartite matching network runs in O(|V| · |E|) because the max flow is bounded by |V| (each augmenting path increases flow by exactly 1). The faster Hopcroft-Karp algorithm achieves O(√|V| · |E|).

With bipartite matching understood, we now generalise: what if vertices are not passive relay points, but have explicit demands — they must receive or supply a certain amount of flow? What if edges must carry at least some minimum amount? These generalisations lead to the circulation with demands model.

9.4.1 Circulation with Demands

Motivation

In the standard max-flow model, there is a single source s that generates flow and a single sink t that absorbs it. Every other vertex is a passive relay: whatever comes in must go out. But many real-world situations require a richer model:

  • A city (vertex) requires a net inflow of 5 units of goods per day — it is a net consumer.
  • A factory requires a net outflow of 3 units — it is a net producer.
  • A transit hub requires exact balance — what enters must leave.

The circulation with demands model captures all of these simultaneously. There is no designated source or sink; every vertex has an associated integer demand du that specifies its required net flow imbalance.

Definition — Demand du
  • du > 0: vertex u has a demand of du units. It must receive du more units on its incoming edges than it sends out. (Net consumer / sink-like.)
  • du < 0: vertex u has a supply of -du units. It must send out -du more units than it receives. (Net producer / source-like.)
  • du = 0: standard flow-conservation — whatever enters must leave. (Pure relay node.)

Formal Problem Statement

Problem Statement — Circulation with Demands

Let G = (V, E) be a directed graph with integer edge capacities c(u,v) and integer vertex demands {du}. A circulation with demands is a function f : V × V → R+ satisfying:

  • Capacity constraint: 0 ≤ f(u,v) ≤ c(u,v) for all edges (u,v).
  • Demand constraint: For every vertex u ∈ V,

        ∑v f(v,u) − ∑v f(u,v) = du

    where f(u,v) = 0 if (u,v) ∉ E.

This is a feasibility problem: we ask whether a valid circulation exists, not how to maximise or minimise anything.

Necessary Condition for Feasibility

Theorem — Necessary Condition

A necessary condition for a feasible circulation to exist is:

∑u ∈ V du = 0

This says the total supply must equal the total demand across all vertices. If this condition fails, no feasible solution can exist (there would be a global imbalance of flow). However, this condition is not sufficient — even when the sum is zero, the graph topology may prevent a feasible circulation.

9.4.2 Reduction to Standard Max-Flow

The key insight is to reduce the circulation with demands problem to a standard max-flow problem on a supergraph G'. The idea exploits the observation that a demand at a vertex is essentially a promised flow that needs to be "funded" by a new super-source, and a supply at a vertex is essentially a promised flow that needs to be "absorbed" by a new super-sink.

Construction of the Supergraph G'

Supergraph Construction (Algorithm)
  1. Keep all original vertices and edges. The original edges retain their capacities. The vertices lose their demand labels (demands are encoded structurally instead).
  2. Add a super-source s*.
  3. For each vertex u with du < 0 (a supplier/producer): add an edge s* → u with capacity -du. This edge represents the supply that u promises to inject into the network.
  4. Add a super-sink t*.
  5. For each vertex u with du > 0 (a consumer): add an edge u → t* with capacity du. This edge represents the demand that u must absorb from the network.

Feasibility Criterion

Theorem — Feasibility via Max-Flow

Define D = ∑u: du>0 du (the total demand, which equals the total supply when the necessary condition holds).

A feasible circulation with demands {du} in G exists if and only if the maximum flow from s* to t* in the supergraph G' has value exactly D.

Equivalently: a feasible circulation exists if and only if the max-flow in G' saturates all edges out of s* and all edges into t*.

Why this works — Proof Sketch

(⇒) If a feasible circulation f exists in G: Define a flow f' in G' by setting f'(s*, u) = -du for all supply vertices, f'(u, t*) = du for all demand vertices, and f'(u,v) = f(u,v) for all original edges. Every new edge is fully saturated, so |f'| = D. This is a maximum flow since the cut {s*} vs the rest has capacity exactly D.

(⇐) If the max-flow in G' has value D: All outgoing edges of s* must be saturated (because their capacities sum to exactly D). Similarly, all incoming edges of t* are saturated. Delete s*, t* and all their incident edges from f'. The remaining flow on original edges is a valid circulation in G: each original vertex v still satisfies the demand constraint because the s* and t* edges were the only imbalance terms.

Key Insight

The super-source s* "funds" all the supplies, and the super-sink t* "collects" all the demands. If every promised supply and demand can be simultaneously honoured, the max-flow saturates all the s* and t* edges. Any shortfall in the max-flow means some vertex's demand or supply cannot be met simultaneously with the capacity constraints.

Full Algorithm

Algorithm 61 — CIRCULATION (Solve Circulation with Demands) Time: O(D · |E|) via Ford-Fulkerson 
function CIRCULATION(G = (V, E), {d_u}):

  // Step 1: Check necessary condition
  if sum of all d_u != 0:
      return INFEASIBLE

  // Step 2: Build supergraph G'
  Create G' as a copy of G (with original edges/capacities)
  Add super-source s*  and  super-sink t*

  for each vertex u in V:
      if d_u < 0:                       // u is a supplier
          add edge (s* -> u) with capacity = -d_u
      elif d_u > 0:                     // u is a consumer
          add edge (u -> t*) with capacity = d_u
      // if d_u == 0: do nothing (u stays a pure relay)

  D = sum of capacities on edges leaving s*
    = sum of d_u for all u where d_u > 0

  // Step 3: Solve max-flow in G'
  f'_max = FordFulkerson(G', source=s*, sink=t*)

  // Step 4: Check saturation condition
  if |f'_max| != D:
      return INFEASIBLE

  // Step 5: Extract the circulation
  Delete s*, t* and all their incident edges from f'_max
  Let f = the remaining flow on original edges of G

  return f   // f is a feasible circulation in G

Step 1 — Necessary condition check: -4 + (-3) + 2 + 0 + 5 = 0. Pass.

Step 2 — Build G':

  • dy = -3 < 0 → add edge s* → y with capacity 3
  • dv = 2 > 0 → add edge v → t* with capacity 2
  • dz = 0 → no new edges for z
  • dw = 5 > 0 → add edge w → t* with capacity 5

So D = 2 + 5 = 7. Also D = |-4| + |-3| = 7. Consistent.

Step 3 — Run Ford-Fulkerson on G'.

The max-flow found has value 7. The flows on all original edges plus the new s* and t* edges:

  • s* → x: 4/4 (saturated)
  • s* → y: 3/3 (saturated)
  • x → y: 2/3, x → v: 2/3
  • y → v: 2/2, y → z: 2/2 (both saturated)

Complexity Analysis

The total capacity out of s* equals D (the total demand). Ford-Fulkerson finds a flow of value at most D in at most D augmentations, each taking O(|E|) time with BFS/DFS. Therefore the total runtime is O(D · |E|). If we use Edmonds-Karp (BFS-based augmentation) the bound becomes O(|V| · |E|2) independent of D.

9.4.3 Circulation with Demands and Lower Bounds

For many applications, it is also necessary to enforce lower bounds on edge flows: each edge must carry at least some minimum amount of flow in addition to being bounded above by its capacity. This models mandatory minimum throughputs — for example, a pipe that must carry at least some flow to stay functional, or a flight that must be serviced by exactly one aircraft.

Definition — Circulation with Demands and Lower Bounds

Each edge (u,v) now has both a lower bound ℓ(u,v) ≥ 0 and a capacity c(u,v) ≥ ℓ(u,v). A valid circulation must satisfy:

  • Modified capacity constraint: ℓ(u,v) ≤ f(u,v) ≤ c(u,v) for every edge.
  • Demand constraint: (same as before) ∑v f(v,u) − ∑v f(u,v) = du for every vertex.

This remains a feasibility problem. We use the shorthand ℓ/c for edge labels (lower bound / capacity), or ℓ/f/c (lower bound / flow / capacity) when showing a solution.

Reduction to Circulation Without Lower Bounds

The strategy is to decompose the required flow into two parts:

  1. A fixed flow fℓ that satisfies all lower bounds exactly: set fℓ(u,v) = ℓ(u,v) for every edge. This flow is mandatory — it will always be sent.
  2. An additional flow f* on top of fℓ, such that the total f = fℓ + f* satisfies the original constraints.

Because fℓ has already committed to the lower bounds, the remaining problem f* is a circulation with no lower bounds but on an adjusted graph G* with adjusted capacities and demands.

Computing the Adjusted Graph G*

Adjusted Graph G* Construction

The vertices of G* are the same as G. For each vertex u, the adjusted demand is:

du* = du − ∑v fℓ(v,u) + ∑v fℓ(u,v)

In words: the fixed flow fℓ already contributes some inflow and outflow at each vertex; the adjusted demand accounts for this by subtracting what the fixed flow has already provided.

The edges of G* are the same as G, but each edge (u,v) now has:

  • Capacity: c*(u,v) = c(u,v) − ℓ(u,v)
  • Lower bound: 0 (no lower bounds in G*)

The rationale: fℓ has "used up" ℓ(u,v) of the capacity, so only c(u,v) − ℓ(u,v) remains for f*. And since fℓ already satisfies the lower bound, f* needs no lower bound constraint.

Theorem — Reduction Correctness

The original circulation with demands {du} and lower bounds {ℓ(u,v)} in G has a feasible solution if and only if the adjusted circulation with demands {du*} and no lower bounds in G* has a feasible solution.

If G* has a feasible solution f*, then the solution to the original problem is f = fℓ + f* (add the fixed lower-bound flow to f* edge by edge).

Step-by-Step Method

Algorithm — CIRCULATION WITH LOWER BOUNDS Two-phase reduction 
function CIRCULATION_WITH_LB(G = (V, E), {d_u}, {ℓ(u,v)}, {c(u,v)}):

  // Phase 1: Fix the lower-bound flow
  For every edge (u,v): f^ℓ(u,v) = ℓ(u,v)

  // Phase 2: Compute adjusted graph G*
  For every vertex u:
      in_ℓ(u)  = sum of ℓ(v,u) for all incoming edges
      out_ℓ(u) = sum of ℓ(u,v) for all outgoing edges
      d*_u     = d_u - in_ℓ(u) + out_ℓ(u)

  For every edge (u,v):
      c*(u,v)  = c(u,v) - ℓ(u,v)    // reduced capacity
      ℓ*(u,v)  = 0                   // no lower bound

  // Phase 3: Solve the no-lower-bounds circulation on G*
  result = CIRCULATION(G*, {d*_u})   // using the algorithm from §9.4.2

  if result == INFEASIBLE:
      return INFEASIBLE

  // Phase 4: Recover the full solution
  f*(u,v) = result for each edge
  f(u,v)  = f^ℓ(u,v) + f*(u,v)    // add the fixed flow back

  return f

Understanding the Adjusted Demand Formula

The adjusted demand du* = du − inℓ(u) + outℓ(u) has an intuitive meaning: the fixed flow fℓ has already contributed inℓ(u) units of inflow and outℓ(u) units of outflow to vertex u. The residual demand du* is what still needs to be satisfied by the additional flow f*.

If fℓ happens to over-satisfy demand at some vertex (more inflow than needed), then du* may flip sign: a vertex that was a consumer in the original problem may become a supplier in the adjusted problem, and vice versa.

After computing G*, solve CIRCULATION on G* (no lower bounds, using the super-source/super-sink method from Section 9.4.2). If G* has a feasible solution f*, then the original problem is solved by f = fℓ + f*. If G* is infeasible, so is the original.

Common Pitfall

When checking the necessary condition ∑ du = 0, use the original demands, not the adjusted ones (the adjusted demands will also sum to zero if the original ones do, but checking on adjusted demands is redundant and can cause confusion).

Also: the edge label notation in the notes uses [ℓ/c] for a problem instance and [ℓ/f/c] for a solution. Do not confuse these.

§ Applications Applications of Network Flow

The full power of the network flow framework — max-flow, min-cut, bipartite matching, and now circulation with demands and lower bounds — lies in its applicability as a universal reduction target. An enormous variety of combinatorial optimisation and feasibility problems can be transformed into one of these flow problems and then solved efficiently using Ford-Fulkerson or related algorithms.

The Reduction Paradigm

The general strategy for applying network flow:

  1. Model: identify entities (vertices), flows (edges), constraints (capacities/demands/lower bounds).
  2. Transform: build the flow network that encodes the problem.
  3. Solve: run the appropriate flow algorithm.
  4. Interpret: read off the solution from the flow values.

The integer-valued solutions produced by Ford-Fulkerson on integer-capacity graphs are especially valuable: they correspond directly to discrete combinatorial choices (assign or don't assign, fly or don't fly, ask or don't ask).

App. 1 Survey Design

Problem Statement

A company sells products P1, ..., Pm and wants to design a customer survey. The customer database has customers C1, ..., Cn. A customer can only be asked about a product they have previously purchased.

There are two types of constraints:

  • Questionnaire size constraint: customer Ci must be asked about between Ci− and Ci+ products (where Ci− is the minimum and Ci+ is the maximum number of questions for that customer).
  • Feedback size constraint: product Pj must be asked about by between Pj− and Pj+ customers.

Question: Can a survey be designed that satisfies all constraints?

Reduction to Circulation with Demands and Lower Bounds

This is a feasibility problem. All vertex demands are zero (the survey design problem doesn't have supply/demand at nodes — it's purely about flow-through constraints). The key constraints are on edge flows.

Graph Construction

Survey Design Network
  1. Add a node Q representing the pool of all questions to be allocated.
  2. Add one node per customer Ci. Add an edge Q → Ci with lower bound Ci− and capacity Ci+. This enforces the questionnaire size constraint.
  3. Add one node per product Pj. For each product Pj that customer Ci has purchased, add an edge Ci → Pj with lower bound 0 and capacity 1. (Either ask the customer about this product, or not.)
  4. Add a node F representing the feedback aggregation. For each product Pj, add an edge Pj → F with lower bound Pj− and capacity Pj+. This enforces the feedback size constraint.
  5. Add a cycle-closing edge F → Q with lower bound ∑ Ci− and capacity ∑ Ci+ (the overall total number of questions). This edge creates a proper circulation by closing the flow cycle.

All vertex demands are set to 0.

Interpretation

Solve the circulation with demands (all zero) and lower bounds problem on this network. A feasible solution corresponds to a valid survey:

  • Flow on edge Ci → Pj equals 1 means customer Ci is asked about product Pj.
  • Flow on edge Ci → Pj equals 0 means they are not asked.
  • The lower/upper bounds on Q→Ci and Pj→F edges enforce the questionnaire and feedback size constraints respectively.

If no feasible circulation exists, the company cannot design a survey meeting all constraints.

Why Integer Solutions Matter Here

Because all capacities and lower bounds are integers, Ford-Fulkerson generates integer flows. On the Ci → Pj edges (capacity 1), the integer flow is either 0 or 1 — exactly what we need to represent a binary "ask/don't ask" decision. This is the integral flow theorem at work: if all capacities and bounds are integers, then a feasible solution with integer flows exists whenever any feasible solution exists.

App. 2 Airline Scheduling

Problem Statement

A company has k airplanes and a set of j desirable flights to operate during a day. Each flight has a specified origin airport, destination airport, departure time, and arrival time. For each pair of flights (i, i'), we are given whether flight i' is reachable from flight i (i.e., there is enough time between the arrival of flight i and the departure of flight i' for a plane to reposition if necessary).

Planes can start and finish the day at any airport.

Question: Can all j desirable flights be covered with only k planes?

Worked Example (from Lecture 11)

  • Flight 1: SYD 6am → MEL 7am
  • Flight 2: CBR 8am → SYD 9am
  • Flight 3: MEL 11am → BNE 1pm (reachable from flights 1 and 2)
  • Flight 4: PER 11am → SYD 7pm (reachable from flight 1)

With k=2 planes, one valid assignment is: Airplane 1 flies (1, 4) and Airplane 2 flies (2, 3).

Reduction to Circulation with Demands and Lower Bounds

Graph Construction

Airline Scheduling Network
  1. For every desirable flight i: add a departure node (departure airport + time) and an arrival node (arrival airport + time). Connect them with an edge of lower bound 1 and capacity 1. This enforces that every desirable flight must be flown by exactly one plane.
  2. Add a start-of-day node S with demand dS = -k (it supplies k planes). For every departure node, add an edge S → departure_i with lower bound 0 and capacity 1. (A plane may start with any flight.)
  3. Add an end-of-day node F with demand dF = k (it absorbs k planes). For every arrival node, add an edge arrival_i → F with lower bound 0 and capacity 1. (A plane may end with any flight.)
  4. Idle plane edge: add edge S → F with lower bound 0 and capacity k. This allows planes to stay idle if not all k planes are needed to cover the flights.
  5. Reachability edges: for each pair of flights (i, i') where flight i' is reachable from flight i, add an edge arrival_i → departure_{i'} with lower bound 0 and capacity 1. (A plane that just landed from flight i can go on to fly flight i'.)

All other nodes (departure and arrival nodes) have demand 0.

Feasibility Check

Solve the circulation with demands and lower bounds on this network.

  • If feasible: the schedule exists. A flow of 1 on a reachability edge arrival_i → departure_{i'} means some plane flies flight i then flight i' consecutively. A flow of 1 on S → departure_i means some plane starts its day with flight i. A flow of 1 on arrival_i → F means some plane ends its day at flight i's destination.
  • If infeasible: it is not possible to cover all j desirable flights with only k planes.
Why the Lower Bound of 1 on Flight Edges is Essential

The lower bound of 1 on each flight edge departure_i → arrival_i mandates that every desirable flight is covered. Without this lower bound, the algorithm could trivially report feasibility by simply not flying any flights. The lower bound is the key modelling choice that encodes "every flight must be flown."

The idle edge S → F with capacity k is also important: if fewer than k planes are needed, the remaining planes "flow" directly from S to F without flying any desirable flight — the idle edge absorbs this slack.

Complexity

The graph has O(j) nodes and O(j2) edges (reachability edges in the worst case). The total demand is k. The circulation algorithm runs in O(k · j2) via Ford-Fulkerson, or O(j2 · (j2)) via Edmonds-Karp.

App. 3+ Further Applications of Network Flow

Project Selection (Closure Problem)

A company has a set of potential projects. Each project either generates a profit (if undertaken) or incurs a cost. Additionally, there are dependencies: undertaking project A may require also undertaking project B (because A depends on B's infrastructure, results, etc.).

Goal: Select a subset of projects to maximise total profit, subject to dependency constraints.

Reduction to Min-Cut

This is the closure problem (also called the project selection problem):

  1. Add a source s and a sink t.
  2. For each project with a positive profit pi: add an edge s → projecti with capacity pi.
  3. For each project with a negative profit (cost) ci: add an edge projecti → t with capacity |ci|.
  4. For each dependency "project A requires project B": add an edge projectA → projectB with capacity ∞.

Find the minimum cut in this network. The projects on the source side of the min-cut (in the S-partition, not adjacent to t via cut edges) are the ones to undertake.

The maximum profit is: (sum of all positive profits) − (value of the minimum cut).

Intuition

Cutting the edge s → i means "we forgo project i's profit." Cutting the edge j → t means "we pay project j's cost." The ∞-capacity dependency edges ensure that if you select a project, you must also select all projects it depends on (cutting an infinite-capacity edge is infinitely costly, so the algorithm never cuts these).

Sport Tournament Elimination

A sports tournament is in progress. Given the current standings and the remaining schedule of games, determine whether a specific team T can still finish as the overall leader (or at least tied for the lead).

Reduction to Max-Flow

Suppose team T currently has wT wins, and there are gij remaining games between teams i and j. Team T is mathematically eliminated if even with all remaining wins going to T, some other subset of teams must collectively exceed T's potential win total.

  1. Create a node for each remaining game (i,j) and a node for each other team i.
  2. Connect source s to each game node (i,j) with capacity gij.
  3. Connect each game node (i,j) to team nodes i and j with capacity ∞.
  4. Connect each team node i to sink t with capacity max(0, wT + remainingT - wi), which is the maximum number of additional wins team i can have without exceeding T's potential maximum.

Team T is eliminated if the max-flow does not saturate all edges out of s (i.e., not all remaining games can be distributed without some team beating T's maximum possible wins).

Image Segmentation (Background/Foreground Separation)

Given an image represented as a graph of pixels, separate the image into foreground and background regions. Each pixel has a likelihood of being foreground vs background, and adjacent pixels have a "separation penalty" (high if they look similar — splitting them is undesirable).

Reduction to Min-Cut

  1. Add source s (represents "foreground") and sink t (represents "background").
  2. For each pixel p: add edge s → p with capacity equal to the likelihood of p being foreground, and edge p → t with capacity equal to the likelihood of p being background.
  3. For each pair of adjacent pixels (p, q): add edges p → q and q → p with capacity equal to the separation penalty between p and q.

Find the minimum s-t cut. Pixels in the S-partition (source side) are labelled foreground; pixels in the T-partition (sink side) are labelled background.

The min-cut minimises: (sum of forfeited foreground likelihoods) + (sum of forfeited background likelihoods) + (separation penalties for adjacent pixels assigned to different regions). This simultaneously favours assigning pixels to their most likely label and penalises splitting visually similar neighbouring pixels.

Path Covers in Graphs

Given a directed acyclic graph (DAG), find the minimum number of vertex-disjoint paths that cover all vertices (each vertex appears in exactly one path).

Reduction to Bipartite Matching

  1. Split each vertex v into two copies: vout (left side) and vin (right side).
  2. For each edge (u,v) in the DAG, add an edge uout → vin in the bipartite graph.
  3. Find the maximum matching M in this bipartite graph.
  4. Minimum number of paths = |V| − |M|.

Each matched edge represents "vertex u is immediately followed by vertex v in a path." An unmatched vertex on the left starts a new path; an unmatched vertex on the right ends a path.

Open-Pit Mining

An open-pit mine is modelled as a grid of blocks. Each block has an associated profit (positive for ore blocks, negative for rock removal cost). Extracting a block may require first removing blocks above it. This is the closure problem again: select a subset of blocks to maximise profit subject to precedence constraints — exactly the project selection min-cut reduction.

Hierarchy of Models (from simplest to most general)

Model Hierarchy
  1. Standard Max-Flow — single source s, single sink t, edge capacities only, maximise flow value. Solvable by Ford-Fulkerson / Edmonds-Karp.
  2. Bipartite Matching — reduces to max-flow with unit capacities and a specific two-layer structure.
  3. Circulation with Demands — distributed sources/sinks (demands at vertices), no single s or t, feasibility question. Reduces to standard max-flow via super-source/sink.
  4. Circulation with Demands and Lower Bounds — additionally, each edge must carry at least its lower bound. Reduces to circulation without lower bounds by stripping the mandatory lower-bound flow. Most general model.

The chain of reductions means that any problem solvable as a circulation with lower bounds can ultimately be solved by Ford-Fulkerson. This is the unifying power of the network flow framework.

Decision Guide: Which Model to Use?

Problem Characteristics Recommended Model
Single source, single sink, maximise throughput Standard Max-Flow (Ford-Fulkerson / Edmonds-Karp)
Find a bottleneck / minimum disruption Min-Cut (read off from residual graph after max-flow)
Assign items from one set to another (one-to-one) Bipartite Matching
Multiple sources/sinks with supply/demand values Circulation with Demands
Assignments/flows with mandatory minimums Circulation with Lower Bounds
Select a subset to maximise profit under dependency constraints Min-Cut / Closure Problem
Scheduling problems (e.g., must cover all tasks with limited resources) Circulation with Lower Bounds (lower bound 1 on task edges)

Key Theorems to Remember

Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem

For any flow network, the maximum value of an s-t flow equals the minimum capacity of any s-t cut. Proved by showing that when Ford-Fulkerson terminates (no augmenting path in residual graph), the set of vertices reachable from s in the residual graph forms an s-side min-cut.

Integrality Theorem

If all edge capacities and lower bounds are integers, then there always exists a maximum flow (or feasible circulation, if one exists) with integer flow values on every edge. Ford-Fulkerson with integer capacities always produces an integer-valued flow.

This is critical for applications: it means integer formulations always give discrete solutions (0/1 assignments, whole unit flows) without needing to round fractional solutions.

Feasibility Theorem for Circulation with Demands

A feasible circulation with demands {du} exists in G if and only if:

  1. ∑u du = 0 (necessary condition), AND
  2. The max-flow in the supergraph G' (with super-source s* and super-sink t*) equals D = ∑u: du>0 du (sufficient condition).
Exam Tips
  • Always check the necessary condition ∑ du = 0 first. If it fails, immediately return infeasible.
  • For lower bounds: the reduction strips lower bounds by fixing them, then adjusting demands. The adjusted demand formula is d*u = du − inℓ(u) + outℓ(u).
  • Saturation condition: in the supergraph, all edges from s* must be saturated AND all edges into t* must be saturated for feasibility.
  • For application reductions: identify what represents a "unit of flow" (a plane, a question, a match), what the capacity constraints mean physically, and what lower bounds enforce. The graph construction follows naturally.
  • Integer-valued flows: always exploit integrality — if capacities and lower bounds are integers, the algorithm produces integer flows, which correspond to clean combinatorial decisions.
